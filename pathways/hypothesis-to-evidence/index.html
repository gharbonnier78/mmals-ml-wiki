Evidence case study

Measurement is not identification

A 2026 Science Advances experiment used a cold-atom interferometer with one matter-wave packet static in the laboratory frame and another in free fall. The observed relative phase agreed with the predicted behavior, supporting the published, bounded conclusion that the result is consistent with applying the equivalence principle in the tested low-energy quantum regime.

What it does not establish: the experiment does not show that gravity itself is quantized and does not uniquely settle every interpretation of the measured phase.

Why this matters for evidence reasoning: before final publication, Asenbaum and Overstreet proposed a competing magnetic-recoil interpretation of the same observable. Dobkowski and colleagues replied that the interferometer remains sensitive to a uniform gravitational field. The disagreement is useful precisely because an observed signal and the mechanism inferred from that signal are different evidential objects.

The transferable rule is: observed predicted signal ≠ uniquely identified explanation. After asking whether the expected observable was measured, ask whether credible alternative models predict it too and what additional measurement or intervention would discriminate among them.

Primary source: Dobkowski et al., Science Advances (2026). Context: original 2025 preprint, technical comment, and author reply. The curated provenance notes are retained under research/sources/.
